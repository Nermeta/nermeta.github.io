## What It's About
Countdown to Zero Day is a book written by Kim Zetter about the Story of Stuxnet. The first Virus of it's kind, it was used in a joint effort by the US and Isreal as an attack on Iran's Nuclear program. This Book recounts the story of how the virus was discovered and its broader implications for the future of Cyber Warfare.

## What I Appreciated
I started reading this book because I have been a long time listener of the Darknet Diaries podcast by Jack Rhysider. In one of his episodes he did an interview with the author of this book and they went over the whole story. I was curious as to what the book would cover and while it covered much of the same information, I appreciated the pacing of how information is revealed and discussed in this novel. Zetter does not give you all the information at once. Instead he tells the story recursively. First starting with Stuxnet's initial discovery, then passing it off the the researchers who dismantled the code all while zooming out on occasion to give context to some of the theory's being discussed. This allows you to discover information in a similar order to how the researchers discovered it.

## Critique

In reading this book there are a couple of things I noticed that I do think are worth discussing. The book can be split into two parts, the first part is the actual story of Stuxnet and the second part is the potential geo-political ramifications. The first part intriguing as it goes through the whole mystery. While the constant shift in POV was sometimes jarring, it made sense story wise to do so. As you are being passed back and forth between the researchers trying to figure the virus out, the consequences of the virus on Iran's centrifuges, and the occasional zoom out to the political landscape. You get the sense of how big a deal this actually was and the amount of work that went into not only creating the virus but deciphering it as well. 

Once you get to the second part however there is no narratives anymore and reads closer to a who's who of politics and nation states. Which if you are into that sort of thing go for it, but for me it's the same story you get with any new weapon. Who can use it against you, what are the rules surrounding it, who has the capability, on and on. Which in of itself can make for some intrigue but it was written in such a way that it felt more like listing facts. This person thinks this, this person thinks that, this person is worried about this, this person thinks it's not that big a deal. It was weaker then the first half of the book which made it a bit of a slog to get through.

## Who Should Read It
This book is written for two types of people; those who like history, and those who like politics. If you want to know more of the technical side of what was going on here you will be disappointed. It gives an overview of the exploits used and a basic description, but for anything more than that you will need to look at the reports that where published on it. 

I do think it gives some good context for many of the concerns today in Cybersecurity and is a good read. If you are a technology enthusiast, or into politics this would be a good book to pick up.
## My Rating
Overall I gave this book a 4. It was extensively researched, well written and for the most part very engaging. I do wish the ending was stronger, but I enjoyed the recursive format and the accessibility of the text for those who may only have a basic understanding of how computers work.
